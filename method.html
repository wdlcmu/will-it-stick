How we test this tool

Will It Stick? tests your words before you send them. This page is how we test the tool itself, with every run printed.

The claim being tested

The question is narrow. Given the same words and the same job, does the panel's answer beat a plain AI answer? We run the test, print the runs, and let the spread show. One run is never a record.

The two sides

The panel side runs the production checker, the same tool visitors use, with its dated limitation line in place.

The plain AI side runs gpt-4o, a ChatGPT class model. It gets the same job in its prompt: name the core the reader keeps, score all six SUCCESs elements from 1 to 10 with reasons, name the weakest element, and give one fix as an instruction. It may not rewrite the words, and it may not add facts.

Both sides get the same ten messages. The ten are frozen in a test kit, so every run answers the same questions. The messages are sample texts, not visitor words.

The judging

Judges score the two answers blind. They see Answer A and Answer B, never which side wrote which, and the A and B order flips from case to case and run to run.

Each case gets two judge passes from gpt-4o-mini. When the two passes split, a gpt-4o adjudicator settles the case. Ties are counted as ties, never as wins.

The judges are models, not people. A benchmark judged by people, on many more messages, is the stronger test. It is planned and not yet run, so this page claims only what the model judged runs show.

What the spread means

The same test, run three times, gave 7, 5, and 7. Run 2 was a coin flip. Scores are draws, and the spread across runs is itself the finding. That is why the homepage prints the spread, not a single best number.

The transformation test, Nov 3-4, 2026

The homepage now leads with a transformation, not a score: the tool scans a draft for its point, writes three transformations of it (a concrete comparison, the one-line core, a scene), drops any whose numbers the draft does not state or plainly derive, and lets cold readers pick the winner by what they carry away. Your original competes, and can win. Before that shipped, the pipeline had to beat both the raw draft and the check's one-fix version on cold-reader outcomes in a 32-draft backtest, and the shipped production version then had to reproduce it. Seven production panels ran across Nov 3-4 as the pipeline changed under steering; pooled, they read 2.80 for the transformed version against 2.61 for the originals and 2.56 for the check's version. The margin is real and modest, and scores are draws here too; the same test in the backtest harness read 3.08 against 2.55 and 2.48.

Two guards keep a transformation honest. A version may carry a kind label (comparison, one line, scene) only if code verifies the device is really there: a comparison construction, a scene anchor, a true compression. Rewrites that only swap words gain about nothing in the published record (Masson and Waldron's rewrite studies), so paraphrases are dropped or labeled plainly as Tightened. And a comparison's numbers are checked against the draft's own numbers before it can ship: a vivid analogy with wrong arithmetic is the most dangerous output a tool like this can produce. When a draft holds nothing concrete to build on, the tool does not improvise: it asks first. Asking beats guessing in the evidence too: across 25 experiments, Eyal, Steffel, and Epley (2018) found that trying to take another person's perspective did not improve accuracy, while asking them (perspective-getting) did. The seed questions and the check's questions-first round are built on that finding.

The winner is picked on gist, on purpose. Readers are asked what the message is saying and what they would repeat, never whether they feel they understood it. That follows Reyna's fuzzy-trace research (people act on the gist they carry, not the verbatim text) and the VA's cued-response testing protocol, which tests what readers actually take away instead of asking them to grade their own comprehension.

What "applied" means in about two runs of three

Every check result carries a dated line: in the Nov 4, 2026 measurements, the server applied the fix itself in about two runs of three (23 of 36, then 26 of 36 on a repeat pass). Applied has a plain meaning here. After the panel names one fix, the server tries to apply that fix to the sender's own words, and two automated guards decide whether the result counts.

The length guard: the new text must stay between 0.4 and 1.6 times the length of the original words.

The correspondence guard: the change in the new text must be the change the fix named. A change the fix never called for fails, and so does a fix the new text never actually made.

When both guards pass, the result shows your words with the fix applied, plus a count of lines changed. When either guard fails, the result carries the fix as an instruction only, with a reason, and no rewritten text. The 36 runs were a fixed set of test inputs measured twice on Nov 4, 2026 against the production server. The numbers describe that set on that day, not a promise about any single check.

The grades

The score layer a reader sees is four plain grades out of 5: easy to read, holds your attention, the point lands, you'd pass it on. Each reader grades them directly in the same cold read, 1 to 5; the result shows the mid grade of the three. They are measured, not worked out from the six scores, and they are the readers' own judgments, never a readability formula. That choice is deliberate: in Kirkpatrick et al. (2017), edited summaries beat the originals on readability formulas but not on human-rated understanding, so a formula score can improve while understanding stands still. This tool never headlines one. The six SUCCESs scores sit underneath as the diagnosis: they drive the questions and the one fix.

Sources

The six scores use the SUCCESs checklist from Made to Stick, by Chip Heath and Dan Heath, backed by Tversky and Shafir (1992), Loewenstein (1994), and Paivio (1971). This tool is independent. The authors and the publisher did not endorse it, and we have no tie to them.
